01 / Modes of convergence

How can random variables converge?

In probability theory, convergence does not have just one meaning. Different modes describe different strengths of closeness between a sequence of random variables and its limit.

Start with a sequence of random variables

Let X₁, X₂, … be random variables defined on a probability space, and let X be a candidate limit. The question is: in what sense does Xₙ approach X as n becomes large?

Xₙ  →  X   as   n → ∞

The four main modes in this homework are convergence in probability, almost sure convergence, mean-square (L²) convergence, and convergence in distribution.

P

Convergence in probability

The probability of deviating from the limit by more than any fixed positive tolerance goes to zero.

P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0
Convergence in measure
a.s.

Almost sure convergence

For almost every outcome ω, the realized sequence converges pointwise to the realized limit.

P(lim Xₙ = X) = 1

Mean-square convergence

The expected squared error between Xₙ and X approaches zero. Large deviations are penalized quadratically.

E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0

Convergence in distribution

The distributions approach the limiting distribution at every continuity point of its CDF.

Fₙ(x) → F(x)

02 / Compare their strength

Which modes imply which others?

The implications matter because the LLN and CLT use different notions of convergence.

Almost sure (a.s.)⇒In probability⇒In distribution
Mean-square (L²)⇒In probability

These are general implications. The reverse implications do not hold in general. Almost sure and L² convergence are not directly comparable without additional assumptions. One useful exception: if the limiting variable is a constant c, convergence in distribution to c is equivalent to convergence in probability to c.

What links a.s. and L²?

Convergence in probability together with uniform integrability of the squares Xₙ² is equivalent to L² convergence (Vitali). So L² convergence needs, in addition to convergence in probability, control of the tails of Xₙ². In the counterexample below, Xₙ = n·1(0,1/n²) converges a.s. but its squares are not uniformly integrable.

Subsequences

If Xₙ → X in probability, there is a subsequence Xnₖ that converges to X almost surely. Hence convergence in probability is "almost" a.s. convergence along a suitable subsequence, which is also how the Borel–Cantelli lemma is used in proofs.

Why does L² imply convergence in probability?

By Markov's inequality applied to the squared error (equivalently, Chebyshev's inequality):

P(|Xₙ−X| > ε) ≤ E[(Xₙ−X)²] / ε²

If the mean-square error tends to zero, the probability of a fixed-size error must also tend to zero.

Why does almost sure convergence imply convergence in probability?

For a fixed ε, consider the event that |Xₙ−X| exceeds ε at some index after N. As N increases, these events decrease to the event of infinitely many such deviations, which has probability zero under almost sure convergence.

Hence the probability of a deviation at a fixed large n tends to zero.

Important: Convergence in distribution is weaker. It concerns the distributions, not necessarily the closeness of Xₙ and X on the same probability space. For example, an i.i.d. Bernoulli(1/2) sequence has the same distribution for every n, so it converges in distribution to Bernoulli(1/2), but it is not Cauchy in probability and therefore does not converge in probability to any random variable.

Almost sure does not imply L²

On [0,1] with a uniform probability measure, let Xₙ = n·1(0,1/n²). For every ω > 0, Xₙ(ω) is eventually zero, so Xₙ → 0 almost surely. But E[Xₙ²] = n²·(1/n²) = 1, so there is no L² convergence.

L² does not imply almost sure

Let Xₙ = 1Aₙ, where the Aₙ are independent events with P(Aₙ)=1/n. Then E[Xₙ²]=1/n → 0, so Xₙ → 0 in L². Since ΣP(Aₙ) diverges, the second Borel–Cantelli lemma gives Xₙ=1 infinitely often almost surely; hence almost sure convergence fails.

03 / The Law of Large Numbers

LLN: averages become stable

Consider independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations with finite mean μ. Their sample average approaches the population mean as the sample size increases.

Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN)

Under suitable assumptions, the sample mean converges to μ in probability:

X̄ₙ = (X₁ + ··· + Xₙ)/n  →ₚ  μ

For i.i.d. variables with finite variance, Chebyshev's inequality gives a direct proof:

P(|X̄ₙ−μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²)

The upper bound tends to zero as n increases.

Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN)

Under suitable conditions, the sample average converges to μ almost surely:

X̄ₙ  → a.s.  μ

This is a stronger statement: with probability one, an individual infinite sample path has its running average converge to the true mean.

For i.i.d. observations with finite first absolute moment, the classical strong law guarantees this almost sure convergence.

Why can the sample mean also converge in L²?

If the observations are i.i.d. with finite variance, the sample mean is unbiased and its mean-square error equals its variance:

E[(X̄ₙ−μ)²] = Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²/n → 0

Thus, under these assumptions, the sample mean converges to μ in mean square, and consequently also in probability. The strong law gives almost sure convergence as well.

04 / The Central Limit Theorem

CLT: fluctuations become normal

The CLT does not say that the unscaled sample mean has a non-degenerate normal limit. It describes the distribution of its properly standardized fluctuations.

For i.i.d. observations with mean μ and finite, positive variance σ², the classical CLT states:

Zₙ = √n (X̄ₙ−μ)/σ  →d  N(0,1)

This is convergence in distribution. It tells us that, for sufficiently large n, probabilities involving the standardized sample mean can be approximated using the standard normal distribution.

LLN versus CLT

ResultQuantityMode
WLLNX̄ₙ → μProbability
SLLNX̄ₙ → μAlmost sure
CLT√n(X̄ₙ−μ)/σDistribution

Why are both needed?

The LLN explains consistency: the estimator gets close to the parameter. The CLT describes the shape and scale of the remaining estimation error, enabling approximate confidence intervals, tests and probability calculations.

Other important results: the Continuous Mapping Theorem transfers convergence through continuous functions. Slutsky’s theorem combines a distributional limit with terms converging in probability. The Delta Method uses a first-order Taylor expansion to obtain the asymptotic distribution of smooth transformations of estimators. These results are fundamental in large-sample statistical inference.

05 / The square-root-of-n scale

Why does √n appear so often?

The square-root scaling is a direct consequence of how variances add for independent observations and how sums fluctuate around their expected values.

Step 1 — Additivity of variance

For two random variables:

Var(X+Y) = Var(X)+Var(Y)+2Cov(X,Y)

Independence is sufficient to make the covariance zero (uncorrelatedness is enough for this variance identity). For n independent observations with common variance σ²:

Var(Sₙ) = Var(ΣXᵢ) = nσ²

Step 2 — Standard deviation

Variance grows linearly with n, but standard deviation is the square root of variance:

SD(Sₙ) = √(nσ²) = σ√n

Therefore, the natural scale of random fluctuations in a sum of independent observations is √n, not n.

Step 3 — Divide by n to obtain the sample mean

Var(X̄ₙ) = Var(Sₙ/n) = (nσ²)/n² = σ²/n
SD(X̄ₙ) = σ/√n

The sample mean becomes more stable as n grows. Its typical error shrinks at the rate 1/√n. Multiplying the centered error by √n brings the fluctuations back to a non-zero, finite scale.

Statistical interpretation: to halve the standard error, we generally need four times as many independent observations, because σ/√(4n) = (1/2)σ/√n.

What if observations are dependent?

Var(Sₙ)=nσ²+2Σi<jCov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ)

Covariance terms need not vanish. Positive correlation can increase the variance of the sum; negative correlation can reduce it. The usual σ/√n standard-error formula cannot be applied blindly to dependent data.

Does dependence always destroy √n scaling?

No. Some dependent sequences still satisfy a CLT under additional assumptions, such as suitable weak-dependence or mixing conditions. The limiting variance may then involve a long-run variance, including autocovariances.

Independence is a convenient sufficient condition for variance additivity and classical LLN/CLT results, but it is not the only possible setting for asymptotic normality.

Why this matters in statistical inference

The square-root scale appears in standard errors, confidence intervals and many estimator asymptotics. For example, if σ is known, a large-sample confidence interval for μ is approximately:

X̄ₙ ± z1−α/2 · σ/√n

For many regular estimators, including maximum-likelihood estimators under suitable regularity conditions, √n times the estimation error is asymptotically normal. This is why the same scale appears far beyond the sample mean.

07 / Conditional probability

Probability changes with information

Conditional probability quantifies the probability of an event when we know that another event has occurred.

Definition

For events A and B with P(B) > 0, the conditional probability of A given B is:

P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

The condition B changes the reference population: instead of considering all possible outcomes, we restrict attention to the outcomes in B.

P(A|B) is generally different from P(B|A). The condition after the vertical bar identifies what is already known.

Multiplication rule

Rearranging the definition gives:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A|B)P(B)

The same joint probability can also be written as P(B|A)P(A). Equating these expressions leads to Bayes' theorem.

P(A|B)P(B) = P(B|A)P(A)

08 / Bayes' theorem

From prior beliefs to posterior probabilities

Bayesian inference combines prior information with the likelihood of observed evidence to produce an updated probability for a hypothesis.

The three fundamental components of Bayesian inference

01 / PRIOR

P(H)

The probability assigned to a hypothesis before observing the new evidence.

02 / LIKELIHOOD

P(E|H)

How probable the observed evidence is if the hypothesis is true. It evaluates the model's compatibility with the data.

03 / POSTERIOR

P(H|E)

The updated probability of the hypothesis after taking the evidence into account.

P(H|E) = [P(E|H) · P(H)] / P(E)

The denominator is the total probability of the evidence, computed with the law of total probability: if H₁,…,Hk form a partition of the sample space with P(Hᵢ) > 0, then

P(E) = Σᵢ P(E|Hᵢ)P(Hᵢ)

With a hypothesis H and its complement the partition has two cells:

P(E) = P(E|H)P(H) + P(E|¬H)P(¬H)

P(E) is also called the marginal likelihood (or evidence): the likelihood averaged over the prior. It does not depend on H, so it acts only as a normalizing constant. This gives the proportional form used throughout Bayesian statistics:

posterior ∝ likelihood × prior   i.e.   π(θ|x) ∝ L(θ;x)·π(θ)

For a continuous parameter θ the marginal likelihood is the integral ∫ L(θ;x)π(θ)dθ.

An equivalent and often intuitive form uses odds:

Posterior odds = Prior odds × Likelihood ratio = [P(H)/P(¬H)] × [P(E|H)/P(E|¬H)]

10 / Likelihood and conjugate updating

A second Bayesian example estimates the unknown probability of heads for a coin using a Beta prior and a Binomial likelihood.

Specify the prior and the data

Let θ be the probability of heads. Begin with a Beta(α, β) prior, then observe s heads and f tails.

Prior: θ ~ Beta(α, β)

11 / Bridge: LLN, CLT and Bayes

Why the prior fades as data accumulate

The Beta–Binomial posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the sample proportion. The weight on the data tends to one, and the posterior spread shrinks on the same 1/√n scale seen in the LLN and CLT.

Posterior mean as a weighted average

With a Beta(α, β) prior and s successes in n = s + f trials:

E[θ|data] = (α+s)/(α+β+n) = w·(s/n) + (1−w)·α/(α+β)
w = n / (n+α+β)

The prior acts like α+β "pseudo-observations". As n → ∞, w → 1 and the posterior mean approaches the sample proportion s/n, which by the LLN converges to the true θ. The prior is eventually washed out.

Posterior consistency and Bernstein–von Mises

Under regularity conditions and a prior with positive mass near the true parameter, the posterior concentrates around the truth (consistency, a LLN-type statement). Moreover, the standardized posterior is asymptotically normal:

√n(θ − θ̂ₙ) | data ≈ N(0, I(θ₀)⁻¹)

This is the Bernstein–von Mises theorem: it connects Bayesian credible intervals with the frequentist CLT-based intervals, and it again involves the scale √n.

How to read them

MAP = argmaxθ π(θ|x) = (α+s−1)/(α+β+n−2)

The MAP is valid when α+s > 1 and β+f > 1; otherwise the mode is on the boundary. With a flat prior Beta(1,1), the MAP coincides with the MLE.

Credible vs confidence interval: a 95% credible interval says that, given the model, prior and data, θ lies in the interval with posterior probability 0.95. A 95% confidence interval is a statement about the long-run coverage of the procedure over repeated samples.

14 / Interpretation

Advantages, limitations and responsible conclusions

Bayesian inference is a coherent way to update uncertainty, but its results depend on the model and prior assumptions.

Advantages of Bayesian inference

  • It combines existing knowledge with new evidence.
  • It produces a full posterior distribution, not only a point estimate.
  • It provides direct probability statements about parameters or hypotheses, conditional on the model and prior.
  • It supports sequential updating as new observations arrive.

Limitations and cautions

  • Results can be sensitive to the chosen prior, especially with limited data.
  • A misspecified likelihood can produce misleading posterior conclusions.
  • Complex posterior distributions may require computational approximation, such as MCMC.
  • A posterior probability is conditional on the model and assumptions; it is not automatically an objective certainty.

Statistical association is not causation

Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem describe how probabilities change when information is observed. They do not, on their own, establish that one event causes another. A medical test result may update the probability of disease; it does not cause the disease. Similarly, an association between training and phishing resistance does not prove that training alone caused the difference. Causal claims require an appropriate study design and assumptions, such as randomization or a credible causal identification strategy.

Always state the conditioning information, the prior, the likelihood model and the population to which the conclusion applies.
